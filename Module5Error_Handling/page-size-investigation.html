How big should a page be?

Make the pieces smaller and you can fit things more closely. Make them larger and there are fewer pieces to keep track of. Both ideas sound sensible. Now you have to choose.

01 · Get the picture

What is a page, exactly?

Imagine storing things in a warehouse that uses one size of box. A small delivery may leave a box partly empty. A large delivery takes several boxes. Uniform boxes make the arrangement easier to manage, but the contents rarely fit perfectly.

There is another expense: someone needs a record of where each box went. Tiny boxes can fit a delivery closely, yet leave you with a great many records. Large boxes reduce that job, but may reserve room that nobody uses.

That gives us a way into the problem. Memory is a collection of numbered storage locations. In a paged system, a program’s virtual address space—the addresses it can refer to—is divided into fixed-size regions called pages. Physical memory, or RAM, has corresponding regions called frames.[1]

Page

A fixed-size region in a program’s virtual address space. It describes a piece of the memory the program sees.

Frame

A page-sized region of physical RAM that can hold a page’s contents. It describes where those contents actually reside.

The order in the program need not be the order in RAM.

Three neighboring virtual pages can occupy three separated frames. The program can still use consecutive virtual addresses. A page table records the mappings; the operating system maintains them, and the processor’s memory-management hardware uses them for translation.[2]

An address identifies a page and a position inside it. Translation finds the frame. The position within that frame stays the same. Accessing a value does not mean copying its whole page somewhere else.

This is useful freedom: the system can find available frames without finding one long, uninterrupted stretch of RAM for the whole program. Mappings also support controlled sharing and protection between processes. Real systems can arrange for pages to become resident when needed.[3]

The bookkeeping lives somewhere.

A page-table entry contains mapping information and status or permission information. Those entries occupy memory. Real page tables have more elaborate structures than our warehouse records, but they are still data the machine has to store.[2]

And a partly filled page is still a whole page.

If an allocation ends partway through its final page, the remainder is unused space inside that allocation. This is called internal fragmentation. The unused part has not vanished; it is simply unavailable for other allocations under that arrangement.[1]

Keep the picture honest.

A page is not a variable, a Rust object, or a Vec. An allocator can put several objects within a page, and a large object can extend across pages. “Page” describes a unit of memory organization.

Real machines have other concerns: translation caches, page-table structure, protection, sharing, and when data is brought into RAM. The page-size decision is not normally an unrestricted choice made by each application.[3] Our exercise gives you a deliberately simpler machine so you can investigate two memory costs carefully.

One unit throughout this exercise: a bit is a single binary digit; a byte contains eight bits. Ordinary machine documentation usually describes page sizes in bytes. Our data, page sizes, and costs are all expressed in bits. Keep the units attached to your numbers.

02 · The problem you are solving

You get one setting.

Suppose you are choosing the page size for a small machine before it is put into service. You have records of the programs it needed to run on 32 different days. Some days may suit one choice better than another. The machine still needs one setting.

Read its description below as a specification. The cost calculation is yours to assemble from it. Notice which information changes when you change page size, and which information is fixed.

This is the entire memory model for the assignment. Use it even where a real operating system would do something more complicated.

The allocation

Whole pages, owned separately

Choose a positive whole-number page size in bits. Every page has that size. Give each program just enough whole pages to hold its requested data. A program’s data can extend across its pages. Programs cannot share pages or borrow the unused part of another program’s allocation.

The records

A record for every page

For each allocated page, the machine stores one 64-bit bookkeeping record, including for a partly filled page. The records live in a separate memory area. Count their bits directly; they do not themselves require pages or further records.

The reserved space

The empty part stays reserved

An allocated page occupies its full size. Space in that page which holds no useful program data remains reserved for that program. Each such unused bit contributes one bit to the memory cost.

The workload

A day is one snapshot

All programs listed for a day are considered together. Score each day independently; do not carry its allocations into the next day. Use the same page size for every program on every day. Assume enough memory exists to run the workloads.

There are no other costs in this model. Ignore execution time, disk activity, allocation history, and memory costs not described here. You are choosing a setting for this model, not changing the configuration of your own computer.

The useful data must be stored whichever page size you choose. The penalty is the extra memory: bookkeeping records plus reserved space that holds no useful data. A day’s score includes the penalties of all its programs.

The overall score is the arithmetic mean of the 32 daily scores. Every day has equal weight. Lower is better. Keep enough precision to distinguish choices; do not round the average to a whole number when comparing them.

Find one page size that minimizes the average penalty across these days. Explain why the evidence supports your choice.

No list of candidate sizes is supplied. Decide what to investigate and explain how you chose it. Be precise about the reach of your conclusion: the best size you have tested and a size you have established is best overall are different claims. If a question remains open, identify it.

Turning an operating description into a model is part of the work. So is finding a way to check that the model says what you think it says.

One choice, evaluated repeatedly

For any page size you consider, apply that choice to every day and average its daily scores. Finding a different favorite for each day answers a different question. You may explore those differences, but your final machine gets one setting.

04 · How to work on it

Leave yourself a trail.

Keep notes in a document or notebook of your choice while you work. A few specific observations will be more useful than a polished story reconstructed at the end.

Use the four steps from class. You can return to an earlier step whenever your understanding changes. Bring substantial work to the peer exchange, even if the problem is not yet solved.

1

Understand the problem.

Clarity of thought · a structural foundation

Before you calculate, put the problem into a form you can see clearly. Separate the data you are given from the choice you control. Identify what the machine promises, what it forbids, and what you are trying to improve.

A sketch, a table, or a short explanation can do the job. Use a form that helps you think. Someone reading it should be able to understand what problem your later calculations are answering.

Questions to organize your picture

  • What does one number in a loads file represent? What does one folder represent?
  • Which quantities are fixed, which are given as data, and which can you choose?
  • What does the machine reserve or store because of your choice?
  • What would count as a better result? What would convince you that you have interpreted the goal correctly?
  • Which parts of the description are you not yet sure you understand?

You may discover later that something here was wrong or incomplete. Keep enough of the first version to see what changed.

KeepYour picture of the problem: its structure, data, constraints, goal, and any open questions.

2

Engage with the problem.

Exploration · persistence · knowing where your understanding stops

Start somewhere you can make progress. Play with an example, draw an allocation, try an extreme choice, make a prediction, or plan a computation. Turn your interpretation into calculations and build a Rust program that can work through the files.

You do not need a complete plan before trying anything. A small attempt may show you what the next useful question is. Keep moving between planning, trying, and checking as far as you can.

At a point worth remembering, jot down

  • What was I trying to understand or make work?
  • What did I expect, and what actually happened?
  • Where, exactly, did I lose the thread?
  • What did I try next, or what help did I seek?

“I was stuck” is a start. “I can compute one program’s cost, but I do not see how to choose which page sizes to test” gives you something to work on. Name the part you can do and the part you cannot yet do.

If things go smoothly, record a check you needed or an assumption you examined. There is no need to invent a failure. Keep enough evidence to explain your current recommendation, or to make your remaining question understandable to somebody else.

KeepSelected attempts, examples, results, and turning points; your current code; and the questions or stuck points you want to bring to the exchange.

3

Share with two peers.

Networking · socialization · cooperation

Choose two people in the class and ask to exchange your thinking. For this take-home assignment, you arrange the conversations yourselves. A group of three works; two separate exchanges work too. You can meet or exchange written work.

Share how you put together Steps 1 and 2, including incomplete work. Receive both peers’ accounts of their own process. Give each person something useful in return: a question, a test worth trying, an explanation, or a connection they might have missed.

Compare the three approaches around the same issue. Three separate summaries leave the comparison for your reader to do. Choose a point where you can put your thinking side by side.

A possible point of comparisonWhat to examine across your work and both peers’ work
Understanding the machineHow did each person represent its rules and costs? Did a sketch or explanation expose something another person missed?
Choosing a starting pointWhat did each person try first? How did that choice affect what they noticed next?
Trusting a resultWhat did each person check? Which claims did the evidence support, and which remained uncertain?
Getting past a difficultyWhere did the thinking stall? What action, information, or conversation helped it move?

Use a concrete detail from your own work and from each peer. Explain why a similarity or difference matters. You may reach the same answer, and you may have similar methods. Look closely enough to say what the comparison taught you without inventing a disagreement.

One of us started with the whole dataset. Another began by drawing one program. What did those starting points let us notice—and what did they leave unanswered?

Return to your investigation after the exchange. Try a suggestion that seems useful, or explain why you decided to keep your approach.

KeepThe names of your two peers, a comparison grounded in all three accounts, useful feedback you gave and received, and what you did with it.

4

Reflect on what changed.

Personal growth · self-awareness · metacognition

Go back to the uncertainties and stuck points you recorded. Connect them to the information you encountered along the way: an experiment, an explanation, a hint, a peer’s question, or a result you had to think about twice.

Describe the connection that helped. “My friend explained it” identifies an event. What did the explanation let you see that you could not see before?

Make a connection you can carry forward

  • I was uncertain about…
  • The information or action that helped was…
  • It helped because…
  • I could use this way of working again when…

A useful habit might be drawing the smallest case, separating quantities that looked alike, checking units, examining an extreme, or asking a more precise question. Choose something that actually mattered in your work and explain when it might help again.

If part of the problem remains unresolved, say what you now understand, what remains uncertain, and what you would investigate next. Your reflection can include an unfinished question.

KeepA few specific connections between your earlier thinking, what you learned, and the problem-solving habits you want to develop.

05 · Bring the work together

Make your choice explainable.

Your final work should let another person follow both the computation and the thinking behind it. Organize it so they can find the four steps without hunting through a transcript of everything you did.

The computation

  • Your Rust source and instructions for running it on the downloaded data.
  • Your recommended page size, clearly labeled in bits.
  • For each day: the number of programs, bookkeeping bits, unused bits, and total penalty at that size.
  • The overall average score and numerical evidence supporting your choice over alternatives.
  • The search or reasoning behind your conclusion, with any limits stated clearly. Report all minimum sizes if you establish a tie.

The thinking

  • Your Step 1 model of the problem.
  • Selected Step 2 attempts, results, and points of uncertainty.
  • Your Step 3 comparison with two named peers, including feedback exchanged.
  • Your Step 4 connections between difficulties, new information, and useful ways of working.

Use concise notes, tables, sketches, or prose as appropriate. Keep the details that help someone understand your reasoning.

The record of your investigation can show that you changed your mind. It can also show why you kept a choice after questioning it. What matters is that the connection between the decision and the evidence is visible.

When somebody asks why you chose that size, you should have more to show them than the number your program printed.
